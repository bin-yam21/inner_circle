- Liquidity = how easily an asset can be bought or sold without moving price much. For ICT: resting buy orders and sell orders.
- Logic: shorts who sold from a swing high keep their stops just above that high → buy-side liquidity (buy stops) above old highs. Longs from a swing low keep stops below it → sell-side liquidity below old lows. Look for where existing orders rest, not for patterns or indicators. (Foundation for "open float", taught later.)
- HIGH-resistance liquidity run: to reach an old significant high, price must travel through lots of earlier price action (many old lows acting as resistance and intermediate highs). Those old highs are well defended; usually only a big catalyst (NFP, FOMC, a black swan) cuts through. Least probable trades — avoid buying into them (mirror for old lows).
- LOW-resistance liquidity run: price comes quickly and one-sidedly off a high with little retracement. After it breaks an old low and then breaks a short-term high, the zone between the break and the violated old low is "low resistance": every new short-term high has buy stops above it and is easy to run. Once price reaches back to the violated low, it becomes high-resistance again.
- Example: a defended longer-term high above → every rally is high-resistance (likely fails), every short-term low gets taken "like a hot knife through butter". Bullish mirror: a defended low → retracements lower are new buys aimed at the buy stops above short-term highs.
- The more price action around a level, the more it is being defended institutionally; trading away from it gives easy runs and small drawdown. Look for "too clean" highs/lows.
